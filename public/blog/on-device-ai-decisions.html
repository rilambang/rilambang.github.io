Shipping on-device AI in an iOS app: the decisions that cost you a rewrite

4 min read · Muhammad Ilham Rilambang

Two decisions get made in week one that decide whether your AI feature is a weekend or a rewrite: the deployment target, and the shape of the branch you write when the model is unavailable.

I am building an AI-native iOS app now — which means learning this in public rather than recalling it, and writing down the parts that turned out to be structural.

Availability is architecture, not error handling

Apple's on-device language model arrives through the Foundation Models framework, which is iOS 26.0+ and watchOS 27.0+. That number is not a preference. Below it, the framework is not degraded — it is absent, at compile time.

So a deployment target is an AI decision now. If your floor is older, "add on-device AI" means either raising the floor for everyone, or maintaining two inference paths from the first commit.

The second half is worse. Apple's documentation is explicit that availability depends on whether the device and region supports Apple Intelligence — and it also documents support for new model versions, which is the polite way of saying the model under you can change without you shipping. Every prompt, every output shape and every evaluation is therefore tied to something you do not version.

That is the argument for treating the unavailable branch as a first-class product path. It is not a spinner and a retry. It is a decision about what the feature becomes on the devices your users actually hold.

The fallback is where the obligation lives

Here is the part that surprised me, and it is a sentence from Apple's review guidelines rather than an inference. Guideline 5.1.2(i):

"You must clearly disclose where personal data will be shared with third parties, including with third-party AI, and obtain explicit permission before doing so."

The operative word is third-party. On-device inference never touches that clause — nothing leaves the device. The moment your availability branch sends a prompt containing anything the user typed to a hosted model, you have entered it: in-app disclosure, explicit permission, the matching App Privacy answers, and the privacy policy to go with them.

Which means the fallback path is not an engineering detail you add in week six. It is the path that carries the legal obligation, and designing it in week one is the difference between a feature and a submission-week problem.

If your feature generates anything, you have four new controls

Guideline 1.2 is about user-generated content, and it names the category explicitly:

"This includes social media, user-generated content platforms, and AI content creation apps."

Apps in that category must include a method for filtering objectionable material from being posted, a mechanism to report offensive content and timely responses to concerns, the ability to block abusive users, and published contact information.

The line is not "does it use AI" — it is what the output is for. A summariser, a code formatter, a private calculation: none of these produce shareable content. A "generate my study plan and share it" feature does, and it inherits all four controls at the moment it gains a share button. Deciding this at the feature boundary is far cheaper than discovering it from a rejection.

What I would do before writing a prompt

  1. Write the deployment target down as an AI decision, and state the consequence: no on-device model below iOS 26.0, no fallback by accident.
  2. Design the unavailable branch first. If it is a third-party model, the disclosure and permission flow is part of the feature, not adjacent to it.
  3. Decide the output's purpose. Kept private, or shareable? If shareable, budget the four controls before the first screenshot.
  4. Assume the model changes. Prompt and output shape are tied to a version you do not control, so keep a small evaluation you can re-run.

How I am working, since the tooling is part of the story

The other half of "AI-native" is the workflow. I run an agent in the editor with skills and plugins, and the rules I have landed on are much less about the agent than about me:

  • The spec comes first, in writing. An agent with a vague brief produces confident code for the wrong problem, and the cost of that is not the code — it is that you review it and keep it.
  • Small diffs, verified by evidence, not by confidence. A hash, a byte count, a test that was red and is now green. A claim I cannot check is a claim I do not accept, from a person or a model.
  • Ask what the change would break, then check that. The failure mode of an agent is not a bad idea; it is an unexamined one that reads well.
  • The boring parts stay mine. Anything touching a permission, a credential, a payment or a privacy surface gets read line by line, and the decision gets made by a person who will still be here next quarter.

None of this is specific to agents. It is the same discipline as review — and it is the reason I like this particular shift: it moves the work toward judgement and away from typing, and judgement is the part that takes years.
