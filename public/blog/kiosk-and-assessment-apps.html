Platform rules

Kiosk and assessment apps: what Apple will and won't let you lock

The hardest problem in a proctored exam app is not the exam. It is that the operating system owns the device, and every restriction you want has to be one Apple already agreed to enforce.

Every team building assessment software has the same first instinct: build the lock. Disable the home button, block the app switcher, kill notifications, watch for screenshots, detect a second screen. It is a long list of interesting engineering, and almost all of it is either impossible or unnecessary.

Two questions decide everything

Before writing a line of anti-cheat, answer these:

  1. Is the device supervised? A supervised device is enrolled and managed by an organisation — the school or the testing centre owns it and can configure it centrally. A student's own iPad is not supervised and never will be.
  2. Does the OS already have a mode for this? If it does, your job is to turn it on correctly, not to reimplement it worse.

Those two questions divide the problem cleanly, and they are the reason a kiosk app is a smaller engineering project than it looks.

What Apple actually provides

For assessment software specifically, Apple ships the Automatic Assessment Configuration framework. Its own summary is the clearest possible statement of scope: "Enter single-app mode and prevent students from accessing specific system features while taking an exam."

Three properties of it are worth knowing before you plan anything:

  • It needs no device management. Apple's own support documentation says securing an iPad or Mac for testing with a supported app "requires no additional setup or management, such as a device management service." An app that adopts this framework locks the device into itself automatically. That is the difference between shipping an exam mode to every student and shipping it only to schools with an MDM.
  • It is the app's decision, not the tester's. The app enables it, and while it is active the user cannot switch to another app. There is no setup flow the exam taker can skip, because there is nothing for them to do.
  • Manual modes win, and that is a trap. If Single App Mode or Guided Access is already enabled on the device, "that feature supersedes assessment mode restrictions." A school that pre-locked the iPads by hand is not getting your app's configuration — they are getting their own, and it may be stricter or looser than you assume.

Assessment mode on iPadOS is supported from iPadOS 9.3.2 onward, and the same framework covers macOS — which matters more than it first appears, because it is the same product decision for two platforms rather than two separate anti-cheat implementations.

The part that is a product decision, not a technical one

Once you know what the OS will do, the remaining work is a policy conversation dressed as engineering:

What do you do with a violation? A framework that prevents an action gives you a clean story. A framework that merely observes one does not. If you cannot prevent it, your options are to warn, to log, or to ignore — and each of those is a different product. Discovery after the exam is not prevention, and telling a candidate they failed because of telemetry they cannot see is not a defensible process.

What do you do when the lock fails? Exam mode is a state the app enters, which means it can also fail to enter — low battery, a crash, a force-quit, a device the OS decided to restart. The honest design assumes the lock will break at least once, and decides in advance what the exam does when it notices. An exam that silently continues in an unlocked state is worse than one that visibly halts.

Who is the fallback for? Assessment mode covers the app. It does not cover a second phone on the desk, a written answer sheet, or a person helping. Every proctoring system has a human layer eventually; deciding where your layer ends is cheaper than discovering it during an incident.

What I'd tell anyone starting this

Read Apple's platform capabilities before designing the countermeasure, because the countermeasures you invent yourself are the ones that get rejected, break on OS updates, or quietly stop working when a device is configured differently than you assumed. The interesting engineering in a proctored exam app is not the lock — it is the exam runtime: offline resilience, autosave that survives a crash, a submission that is idempotent, and a state machine that never loses a candidate's work. The lock is a configuration. Treat it like one, and spend the effort where the candidate actually feels it.
